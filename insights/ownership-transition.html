The ownership transition: why most small-business exits are closures

A large share of America’s small companies will change hands over the next decade. The evidence suggests most owners are unprepared, and that preparation is the variable they control.

Published
November 2026
By
Foreman Research
Topics

Related

McKinsey estimates that some six million small-business ownership transitions are coming by 2035, representing up to $5 trillion of enterprise value. How those transitions end is the open question.

Most exits are closures

McKinsey’s Institute for Economic Mobility examined the roughly 510,000 small and midsize businesses that exited the market in 2022. Ninety-two percent closed. Five percent were sold, and three percent were transferred to new owners.

92%

Share of small and midsize business exits in 2022 that were closures, according to McKinsey’s Institute for Economic Mobility.

Not every closure is a failure. Many are sole proprietorships with little to sell beyond the owner’s own work. But the figure also includes companies with customers, employees and real earnings that never found a buyer or a successor.

Few owners know where they stand

The same research found that fewer than one in three owners has an exit plan, and fewer than one in ten can name their company’s value within 10% of what it is actually worth. An owner who does not know the value cannot judge an offer, and an owner without a plan often runs out of time.

Buyers are paying for quality

Demand has not disappeared. BizBuySell counted 2,132 small businesses sold in the third quarter of 2026, down 7% from a year earlier, while the median sale price rose 17% to $350,000. Service companies made up 47% of transactions, and their median cash flow rose 18% to $157,378.

Fewer sales at higher prices is consistent with buyers concentrating on better-prepared companies: those with documented earnings, limited dependence on the owner and financing that works.

What preparation looks like

  • Know the number. A value range based on normalized earnings and comparable transactions.
  • Test the financing. Whether a typical buyer could borrow enough to pay that price under current lending rules.
  • Reduce dependence on the owner. Delegate key relationships and document how the business runs.
  • Decide about the property. If the owner holds the real estate, settle whether it will be sold, leased or retained.
  • Allow time. Changes like these can take one to three years to show up in the numbers a buyer reviews.
